## Executive summary

Casting is the feature that sends a video, a song or a screen from a phone to a television, a speaker or a display. On the home network it rests on a small set of protocols: a device announces itself over multicast DNS, a controller finds it, and the controller sends commands to a local service on the device. The pattern that runs through this study is the one casting shares with the UPnP port-mapping service covered in a [companion study](/case-studies/UPNP_HOME_NETWORK.md): the local service on the device trusts the local network. Craig Young, who found a location leak in Google's cast devices in 2018, put the underlying assumption as two design choices common to home devices, that they "rarely require authentication for connections received on a local network" and that "HTTP is frequently used to configure or control embedded devices" [2] (**Confirmed**).

The record of what that assumption allowed falls into a few groups. One is taking over a device within wireless range: the 2014 Rickmote, which knocked a Chromecast off its Wi-Fi so that it reconfigured and, in Bishop Fox's words, "accepts commands from anyone within proximity" [1] (**Confirmed**). One is reading or abusing the unauthenticated local control interface: the 2018 location leak, where a web page used DNS rebinding to reach the device's local HTTP service and pull the Wi-Fi survey data that a geolocation service turns into a street address [2] (**Confirmed**), reported by Brian Krebs with the researcher's figure of locations within about ten metres of the device [3] (**Reported**); and the 2021 account-linking issue, where the device's own identifying information, read over the local network, was enough to link an attacker's cloud account and then drive the device remotely, including a wiretap built from a scheduled call, for which Google paid a researcher a total of 107,500 US dollars [5] (**Confirmed**). One is the casting protocols' own weaknesses: DIAL, the discover-and-launch protocol used to play video on many televisions, which a 2019 research project found most television vendors had not implemented correctly [6] (**Confirmed**). And one is what happens when any of this is exposed to the internet rather than kept on the LAN, which is the 2019 CastHack incident [4] [5] (**Reported** for the incident).

The defensive reading sorts the same way as the UPnP study. Some of these are bugs with a dated fix: most of the local cast API began requiring an authorization token in 2019, the account-linking path was closed by the time its researcher published in December 2022, DIAL's protocol gaps were narrowed by a 2020 update from Netflix, and Apple's AirPlay flaws (AirBorne, included here for comparison) were fixed in software released on 31 March 2025 [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] (**Confirmed**). Others were narrowed rather than closed, or are design properties no patch removes: a local service that trusts the LAN behaves as written, and Young's only complete mitigation is to disconnect the devices, with network segmentation among the less drastic steps that reduce exposure. And some is up to the owner: whether the casting network is segmented, whether a receiver that is not used is turned off, and whether firmware is current. A reader can check the state of all three on their own network with read-only steps, which the last Part sets out. None of those checks was run for this study.

## Scope and method

This is a reference study of one attack surface, the local control interfaces behind home casting, read across the public record from 2014 to 2025. It is written for a typical home network: a router or mesh system, a television or streaming dongle, a smart speaker or display, and sometimes a second router behind an internet-service-provider gateway. It is a defensive study. It explains each protocol well enough to show why a problem worked, then what a reader can check and decide, and it reproduces no attack recipe against any specific product and no exploit code. Nothing here was tested against any device; the checks in the last Part are described as read-only steps a reader could take, and none was run.

| Grade | Meaning in this study |
|---|---|
| Confirmed | Stated in a primary opened for this study: the researcher's own write-up, the vendor advisory, or the CVE and NVD record |
| Reported | Known through journalism or a secondary summary |
| Inferred | This study's own reasoning from cited facts, labelled where it appears |

The primaries are Dan Petro's Bishop Fox post for Rickmote [1]; Craig Young's Tripwire write-up [2] and Brian Krebs's report [3] for the 2018 location leak; the archived CastHack page for the 2019 exposure [4], with Matt Kunze's analysis of its likely cause [5]; Kunze's own write-up for the 2021 account-linking issue [5]; Yunus Çadırcı's DIALStranger material for the DIAL weaknesses [6]; Oligo Security's report for AirBorne [7], with the CVE and NVD records [8] and Apple's advisory [9] for the identifiers, scores and fixed versions. Where a statement is a researcher's own claim about reach or impact rather than a measured fact, this study says so.

## Part 1: What casting is, and whether it checks who is asking

Casting is not one protocol but a small stack, and the home has more than one casting system on it at once. The parts that matter for security are discovery, the local control service, and, for televisions, the DIAL launch protocol.

**Discovery.** A cast device announces itself on the local network with multicast DNS (mDNS) and DNS-based service discovery (DNS-SD). A Google Cast device, for example, advertises the service type `_googlecast._tcp`, and a controller browsing for that service finds the device's name and address without any configuration. Matt Kunze's write-up shows exactly this, using the `dns-sd` tool to list a Chromecast, a Nest Hub and a Home Mini on one network [5] (**Confirmed**). DIAL televisions are found a different way, over the Simple Service Discovery Protocol (SSDP), the same UDP discovery used by UPnP: a controller sends an `M-SEARCH` request and DIAL devices answer, which is how the DIAL Scanner in the DIALStranger material enumerates them [6] (**Confirmed**).

**The local control service.** Once found, a Google Cast device exposes a local HTTP service. Kunze found it on TCP port 8008 and, over TLS, 8443, with port 8008 deprecated in favour of 8443, and he read device settings and information straight from it [5] (**Confirmed**). The question that matters is whether that service checks who is asking. Before 2019 it did not. Craig Young's 2018 analysis states that commands "to do things like setting the device name and WiFi connection are sent directly to the device without any form of authentication", and that although the official app implies you must be signed in to a linked account, "there is, in fact, no authentication mechanism built into the protocol level" [2] (**Confirmed**). That is the cast stack's version of the property the UPnP study describes: the service trusts the local network and the inside is assumed friendly.

**DIAL, discover and launch.** DIAL is a separate protocol for the specific job of letting a phone tell a television to open an app and play something. Çadırcı's material describes it plainly: "Discovery and Launch (DIAL) is a protocol co-developed by Netflix and YouTube with help from Sony and Samsung" [6] (**Confirmed**). After SSDP discovery, the controller uses a small HTTP interface on the television to start an application such as YouTube or Netflix and hand it what to play. DIAL is a common casting path on smart televisions, and Part 4 returns to how it was implemented.

The normal, intended use of all of this is harmless and is the reason the design is the way it is: a guest on the home Wi-Fi should be able to cast a video to the living-room television without typing a password into the television. The problems in the rest of this study come from the same place that convenience does, which is that the device answers whoever can reach it on the network, and from what happens when the set of things that can reach it grows beyond the household (**Inferred** from the protocol descriptions [2] [5] [6]).

## Part 2: Taking over a device within range, Rickmote

The earliest problem in the record does not touch the control API at all. It uses the way a cast device recovers when it loses Wi-Fi.

In 2014 Dan Petro of Bishop Fox built the Rickmote, a tool that hijacks a nearby Chromecast and plays a music video on the attached television. The mechanism is in Bishop Fox's own description: the tool "accomplishes this by briefly disconnecting nearby Chromecasts from their Wi-Fi. When this loss of connectivity occurs, the Chromecast tries to reconfigure and accepts commands from anyone within proximity" [1] (**Confirmed**). Bishop Fox's post stops there; the mechanism underneath it, that Wi-Fi is dropped with a deauthentication attack and the device then opens its own setup network, is described by Kunze, who reproduced it on a Google Home Mini (Part 3) [5] (**Confirmed**). The condition the source gives is proximity, not being on the victim's network: an attacker in wireless range can both force the disconnect and reach the recovery state.

Deauthentication works because ordinary Wi-Fi management frames, including the frame that tells a client to drop its connection, are unprotected unless Protected Management Frames (802.11w) are in use, which WPA2 makes optional and WPA3 requires; Kunze records that the Google Home Mini he tested supports neither [5] (**Confirmed**). How current Rickmote is depends on two things. The fall-back-to-setup behaviour is a design property of how cast devices recover, not a one-off bug, and it persisted: Kunze reproduced the disconnect-to-setup-network step and notes that a device can still be forced into setup mode and made to hand over its device information [5] (**Confirmed**). What an attacker can do from there narrowed over time, because the local API later required a token and the device information alone stopped being enough to link an account, as Parts 3 and 7 describe; and on devices with a display Kunze notes the setup network is protected with a password shown as a QR code, which adds a layer of protection [5] (**Confirmed**). So the specific 2014 outcome of casting straight to a stranger's television is not what an updated device does today, but the recovery-state design property remains; this reading rests on the dated changes Kunze records rather than on any source declaring Rickmote dead (**Inferred**).

## Part 3: Reading the unauthenticated local interface

The local control service from Part 1 was reachable without credentials, and two separate pieces of research turned that into real consequences four years apart.

**The location leak, 2018.** Craig Young set out to build a training exercise and found a larger issue. Because the cast device's local HTTP service answered without authentication, a web page open in a browser on the same network could reach it using DNS rebinding, a technique that tricks the browser into treating the attacker's web server and the local device as the same origin. Young used it to hijack the screen attached to a Chromecast and, more seriously, to read the Wi-Fi survey data the device collects, the list of nearby wireless networks and their signal strengths. In his own account the attack located him "with astonishing accuracy", and "About a minute after the page had loaded, I was looking at my house on Google Maps" [2] (**Confirmed**). The precise figure comes from his interview with Brian Krebs, where he said that with the attack demo he had "been consistently getting locations within about 10 meters of the device" [3] (**Reported**). The result is that a web page, with no special permission, could work out the street address of the home it was being viewed in.

Young's own mitigations are the defensive core of this study, and they are structural rather than exploit-specific. His complete answer is to disconnect the devices; short of that, he keeps connected devices on a separate network from the one he browses on, and gives the simple version for ordinary users: add a second router for connected devices, so that, in his words, "attacker code running on the main network will not have a path to abuse those connected devices" [2] (**Confirmed**). He also points to DNS rebind protection, noting that the DNS software in many consumer routers can block the rebinding step though most do not enable it by default or even offer the option, and that "OpenWRT and DD-WRT users can and should enable this protection" [2] (**Confirmed**).

Young published in June 2018 in coordination with Krebs. The fix history is narrower than a single date suggests. Krebs reports that when Young first raised it, Google closed the report with a "Status: Won't Fix (Intended Behavior)" message, and only after Krebs made contact did the company say it would ship an update, then slated for mid-July 2018 [3] (**Reported**). Separately, Kunze's timeline of the platform records that in June 2019 the local cast API began requiring an authorization token; he does not state that this was the fix for the 2018 leak, and he notes that most, not all, endpoints need the token, with the device-info endpoint still answering without one [5] (**Confirmed** for the 2019 token; **Inferred** that it closed the rebinding path this attack used).

**The account-linking wiretap, 2021.** Matt Kunze reported a subtler issue in January 2021 and published it in December 2022, by which time it was fixed. A linked Google account gives broad control of a cast device, including the "routines" feature, which can run voice commands on the device on a schedule. Kunze found that the information needed to link an account, the device's name, certificate and cloud identifier, was served by the local API, and that this information, read over the LAN, was effectively the credential: with it, an attacker could link their own account through Google's cloud and then drive the device remotely. From there, routines gave a wiretap. Kunze built a routine that told the device to call his phone (the command Call followed by a phone number), picked up, and listened to the room through the device's microphone, with the only visible sign being an LED he describes as easily mistaken for a firmware update [5] (**Confirmed**). Because the device information could also be read from the Rickmote-style setup network, an attacker did not even need the Wi-Fi password: force the device into setup mode, read its information, then link the account from anywhere [5] (**Confirmed**).

Kunze tested everything on a Google Home Mini and writes that he assumes it worked similarly on other models, which is his inference rather than a tested result [5] (**Confirmed** for the Home Mini; the other models are the researcher's assumption). Google paid him a total of 107,500 US dollars and deployed the fixes Part 7 lists. The point that carries beyond the specific bug extends one Young had already made in 2018, that data reachable on the local network without credentials is reachable by adversaries [2]: an unauthenticated local service does not only let an attacker send commands, it lets an attacker read identifying data that becomes a key elsewhere, which is this study's own framing of the two cases together (**Inferred**).

## Part 4: The casting protocol's own weaknesses, DIALStranger

The problems above are mostly about Google's cast devices and their local API. DIAL, used to play video on many televisions, carried weaknesses in the protocol itself and in how vendors built it.

Yunus Çadırcı, who also published the CallStranger UPnP issue covered in the UPnP study, researched DIAL in 2019 and released it publicly in 2023 under the name DIALStranger. His summary is direct: "I found protocol doesn't cover some basic security features and most of TV vendors didnt implement protocol correctly. Hackers can play any video on the TVs with or without user interaction" [6] (**Confirmed**). The shape of the problem is familiar from the rest of this study: DIAL answers on the local network, and where a television's DIAL interface does not properly check the origin of a cross-site request, a web page or another device on the network can start apps and play content on the television; this origin-checking weakness and the vendor behaviour are set out in his 2019 paper and 2023 slides [6] (**Confirmed**). His list of the DIAL interface's location across vendors shows the application path is usually `/apps/` but the port varies widely, from LG on 36866 to Samsung on 8001 under `/ws/apps/`, with some rows being game consoles rather than televisions; reading that spread as a sign of uneven implementation is this study's own gloss (**Inferred**).

Çadırcı held the public release for four years, and his stated reason is the same slow-patch problem the UPnP study describes for CallStranger: protocol-level issues take a long time to reach every device. He is explicit that the situation improved but is not closed. On the better side, he records that "Netflix updated protocol 2020 and covered some holes", that responsible vendors updated devices, and that browsers removed features (such as FTP support and WebRTC local-IP disclosure) that had helped attackers find local devices [6] (**Confirmed**). On the unresolved side, he notes that old televisions are not updated and will not be, and he argues that DIAL is not purely a local-network concern because many televisions are exposed to the internet; his own figure for how many varies across his write-ups, from thousands in the 2019 paper to over a million later, and he gives no method, so this is his claim rather than a measured count [6] (**Confirmed** as the researcher's claim). For a reader the practical reading is per-device and per-year: a television patched after 2020, from a vendor that shipped the update, is in a different position from one that stopped receiving firmware before then (**Inferred**).

## Part 5: What happens when casting is exposed to the internet, CastHack

Everything in Parts 1 to 4 assumes the attacker is on the local network or reaching it through a browser on it. In January 2019 a public incident showed what the same interfaces allow when they answer from the internet instead.

Under the name CastHack, two accounts, @HackerGiraffe and @j3ws3r, used internet-exposed Google Cast and smart-television control endpoints to display a message urging viewers to subscribe to a YouTube channel; by the time of the archived page they had stopped playing videos and were, in the page's words, "JUST TESTING FOR EXPOSED DEVICES" [4] (**Reported**). The capabilities the page describes are the ordinary local-API actions seen from the wrong side of the network: it lists being able to "play media on your device, rename your device, factory reset or reboot the device, force it to forget all wifi networks, force it to pair to a new bluetooth speaker/wifi point, and so on" [4] (**Reported**). The page gives no reliable device count (its on-page counters are frozen); the figure of thousands of exposed Cast devices comes from Kunze [5] (**Reported**). This is the clearest link between this study and the UPnP study: a control interface that is safe only because it is supposed to be reachable only from inside becomes dangerous the moment it answers the internet.

The cause is worth stating carefully, because the operators' account and a researcher's differ. The CastHack page itself points at UPnP, telling owners how to fix their devices: "Disable UPnP on your router, and if you're port forwarding ports 8008/8443/8009 then STOP forwarding them" [4] (**Reported**). Kunze, looking at it directly, is more cautious: he writes that "it appears that UPnP is only used by Cast devices for local discovery, not for port forwarding, so the likely cause was a widespread networking misconfiguration (that might be related to UPnP somehow)" [5] (**Confirmed** as the researcher's assessment). So the honest cross-link to the UPnP study is not that UPnP port-forwarding is a proven cause of CastHack, but that CastHack is the same lesson from one or another cause: a local-only control interface exposed to the internet, whether by a UPnP port map or by some other router misconfiguration, is reachable by anyone. Either way it is a networking problem the device vendor cannot patch, which puts it, like a router answering UPnP on its WAN side, in the owner-and-ISP column (**Inferred** [4] [5]).

## Part 6: AirPlay for comparison, AirBorne

Casting is not only a Google and smart-television story. Apple's AirPlay is the same kind of feature, a device announcing itself on the LAN and taking media and commands from controllers, and a 2025 research project shows the same design assumption producing much more serious bugs. It is included here for comparison.

In 2025 Oligo Security published AirBorne, a set of flaws in AirPlay and in the AirPlay software development kit (SDK) that third-party speakers, receivers and CarPlay units use. Oligo states that it "disclosed 23 vulnerabilities to Apple in total, leading to 17 CVEs being issued" [7] (**Confirmed**). The flaws start from the same place as the rest of this study: AirPlay listens on a port (7000) and, in Oligo's account, "many protocol commands were fully accessible in the default settings" [7] (**Confirmed**). Where AirBorne goes further than the cast issues is in impact: Oligo says it demonstrated two of the flaws, CVE-2025-24252 and CVE-2025-24132, "to weaponize wormable zero-click RCE exploits", that is, to run its own code on a device with no user action and spread from one device to the next [7] (**Confirmed**); its table lists five flaws usable to take over a device, two of them marked potential, and it describes a one-click chain as well.

The identifiers and scores here are taken from the CVE and NVD records, not the research summary, because the attribution matters. CVE-2025-24252 is a use-after-free memory flaw (CWE-416); the CVSS 3.1 score of 8.8 attached to its record is CISA's enrichment, not an NVD base score, and its vector `AV:A/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H` has `AV:A`, meaning the attacker must be on the adjacent network [8] (**Confirmed**). Oligo describes chaining it with CVE-2025-24206, a user-interaction-bypass flaw (CWE-288, CISA score 7.7, vector `AV:L`), to reach a zero-click takeover of a Mac, but only where the AirPlay receiver is set to "Anyone on the same network" or "Everyone"; the macOS default is "Current User", so the zero-click chain needs a non-default setting [7] [8] (**Confirmed**). The third, CVE-2025-24132, is a stack buffer overflow in the AirPlay SDK, and here the sources diverge usefully. Oligo says it demonstrated code execution through this flaw on a speaker and on a car multimedia unit, neither named; the CVE record, by contrast, carries NVD's own base score of 6.5 (CWE-119) and describes the effect only as that an attacker "may cause an unexpected app termination", which Apple's own advisory repeats [7] [8] [9] (**Confirmed** for each source's own words). The divergence is not between scores, since Oligo publishes none, but between Oligo's demonstrated code execution and the record's crash, and between the two weakness classifications (Oligo's CWE-121, stack-based overflow, and NVD's CWE-119).

What the research tested and what it reasons about are worth keeping apart. Oligo shows a memory-corruption primitive working on macOS in a video, and presents the speaker and car demonstrations beside it [7] (**Confirmed**); its wider statements about reach are estimates, for example "the number of third-party audio devices that support AirPlay can be estimated in the 10s of millions" [7] (**Confirmed** as Oligo's estimate). Apple released the operating-system fixes on 31 March 2025 (iOS and iPadOS 18.4, iPadOS 17.7.6, macOS Sequoia 15.4, macOS Sonoma 14.7.5, macOS Ventura 13.7.5, tvOS 18.4 and visionOS 2.4), adding the AirPlay CVE entries to those advisories on 28 April 2025 [9] (**Confirmed**). It released the SDK fix to device makers through its MFi programme, AirPlay audio SDK 2.7.1 and video SDK 3.6.0.126 on 31 March 2025 and the CarPlay Communication Plug-in R18.1 on 4 April 2025 [9] (**Confirmed**); a third-party speaker or car unit is fixed only when its maker ships firmware built on that SDK, which is the study's "equipment that stopped getting updates" point in another form. Six of the 17 CVEs were addressed in Apple's January 2025 releases, so these dates apply to the three this study names, not to the whole set [7] (**Confirmed**). The comparison the study draws is that AirPlay is a casting protocol with the same trust-the-LAN starting point as Google Cast and DIAL, and that the starting point is what let a memory bug in it become a same-network takeover (**Inferred**).

## Part 7: What is fixed, what is design, what is a setting

The whole record sorts into three piles, and keeping them apart is the point of the study.

- **Fixed, by a patch with a version or a date.** Most of the local cast API began requiring an authorization token in 2019, though the device-info endpoint still answers without one [5] (**Confirmed**). The account-linking path was closed by the time Kunze published in December 2022: he records that linking an account through the cloud API now requires an invitation to the device's "Home", so that an attacker who only read the device information receives a permission error, and that the remote routine that made the wiretap no longer runs the call command remotely [5] (**Confirmed**). DIAL's protocol gaps were narrowed by Netflix's 2020 update and by per-vendor firmware, unevenly [6] (**Confirmed**). AirPlay's AirBorne flaws were fixed in Apple's 31 March 2025 releases and in the AirPlay SDK released to device makers the same day, with third-party devices fixed only once their makers ship firmware built on it [9] (**Confirmed**). A device that no longer receives firmware stays where it was, which is why an old smart television or an abandoned speaker is the real exposure.
- **Design, which no patch removes, or narrowed rather than closed.** A cast device answers the local network, and it falls back to a recovery state when it loses Wi-Fi. Both are deliberate, because both make the device easy to set up and use. The 2019 token and the 2021 invitation requirement raised the bar on what an unauthenticated LAN or setup-network peer can do, but they narrowed the exposure rather than removing it: the device still trusts the local segment for discovery and basic interaction, and an attacker on that segment, or reaching it through a browser, is inside the trust boundary. DIAL is in the same position, narrowed but not closed, with old televisions left where they were. Young's only complete mitigation for the trust-the-LAN property is to disconnect the device; everything short of that reduces exposure rather than ending it [2] (**Confirmed**).
- **Up to the owner.** Whether casting devices sit on a separate network from the one used for general browsing is the owner's choice. It is Young's first numbered option, though he does not rank his options; calling it the most effective step is this study's judgment (**Inferred** [2]). So is whether a receiver that is not used, such as AirPlay's receiver on a Mac, is turned off or restricted to the current user; Oligo's own four steps are to update, to disable the receiver when it is not in use, to restrict AirPlay's port 7000 to trusted devices, and to set "Current User", with the caveat that this last setting "does not prevent all of the issues mentioned in the report" [7] (**Confirmed** as Oligo's advice). So is keeping firmware current and retiring equipment that no longer gets updates.

The trade-off, as with UPnP, belongs in the decision rather than hidden from it. Putting cast devices on a separate network can break the convenience that casting exists for, because a phone on the main network may no longer see a speaker on the device network, and casting across segments needs either a guest mode or a deliberate exception. That is the owner's call to make; this study describes the setting and its effect, and does not tell anyone which way to set it (**Inferred**).

## Part 8: Check your own network

These checks are read-only. They look at the current state of a network and change nothing, and none of them was run for this study. Deciding whether to segment the network, turn a receiver off, or retire a device comes after, and is the owner's decision.

1. **List the casting devices that answer on the network.** A cast device announces itself over multicast DNS; a controller or a network tool can browse the service types (such as `_googlecast._tcp` for Google Cast) and list what answers. Reading this list tells you which devices are advertising a casting interface on your LAN, which is the first thing any attacker on the network would also enumerate [5] (**Confirmed** that discovery works this way; the enumeration here is described, not run).
2. **Check whether casting devices share a network with general browsing.** Young's structural mitigation is to keep connected devices on a separate network from the one used to browse the web, so a rogue web page or app cannot reach them [2] (**Confirmed**). Read your own setup to see whether that separation exists, for example whether a guest network or a second router carries the connected devices, without changing anything yet.
3. **Check for DNS rebind protection on the router.** The 2018 location leak relied on DNS rebinding, which router DNS software can block. Young notes that OpenWRT and DD-WRT can enable this protection, and that most routers do not enable it by default or even offer the option [2] (**Confirmed**). Read whether your router offers it and whether it is on.
4. **Check that casting interfaces do not answer from the internet.** The CastHack incident was devices answering their control interface to the public internet [4] (**Reported**). A home device should answer casting only on the inside. Confirming the negative from outside is not something a general port checker settles reliably, and where a second router sits behind a gateway the public address is the outer router's; the dependable reading is to know that no port-forward or router misconfiguration exposes the casting ports, which is a read of the router's own forwarding table, the same read the UPnP study describes [4] [5] (**Inferred**).
5. **Check firmware currency and support status per device.** Because the fixes in this study each have a version or a date, a device's exposure depends on whether it still receives updates. Read each casting device's firmware version and whether the vendor still supports it; a television or speaker past its update window stays at whatever year it stopped (**Inferred** from the dated fixes [5] [6] [8]).

What these readings tell you maps onto Part 7: whether the devices are separated from general browsing (a structural choice), whether the router blocks the rebinding step and does not expose the ports (settings and a possible fault), and whether firmware is current (a setting and a support question). None of it requires touching another person's device or sending anything other than ordinary discovery to your own network.

## Cross-cutting themes

- **A local service that trusts the network is the common root.** Rickmote, the location leak, the account-linking issue, DIAL's weaknesses and AirBorne all start from a device that answers the local segment without authenticating the caller. Young stated the fix as a design rule in 2018: local services, especially HTTP ones, "must be designed as if they were directly exposed to the Internet", and that "any data accessible on the local network without credentials is also accessible to hostile adversaries" [2] (**Confirmed**).
- **Reading is as dangerous as commanding.** The 2018 leak and the 2021 account-linking issue both turned on data read from an unauthenticated interface, Wi-Fi survey data in one case and device identifiers in the other, becoming useful somewhere else. An interface that only reveals information is not automatically safe [2] [5] (**Confirmed**; the link between the two cases is this study's framing, **Inferred**).
- **Exposure changes everything.** The same control interface is a convenience on the LAN and a public hazard on the internet; CastHack is the local-only design meeting the open internet through a router misconfiguration [4] [5] (**Reported** for the incident; the cause is the researcher's assessment).
- **A weakness has a year.** Where a fix exists it has a version or a date, so a 2014 or 2018 weakness is not a current one on an updated device; but several of these items were narrowed rather than closed, or are design properties no patch removes, and the real exposure is equipment that stopped receiving updates. Read before you change: every defensive step in this study starts by reading the current state, and the change is the owner's to make (**Inferred**).
