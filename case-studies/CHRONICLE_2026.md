## Executive summary

For the people who defend networks, 2026 was the year the shared tool turned on its users. In twelve days of March, stolen credentials turned the Trivy vulnerability scanner and its GitHub Actions into credential stealers, the compromise travelled through a CI pipeline into two releases of the LiteLLM AI gateway on PyPI, and the axios npm package shipped a remote access trojan to anyone who installed it in a three-hour window. A poisoned VS Code extension then reached a GitHub employee's device and internal GitHub repositories, a worm moved through more than 1,300 npm package versions, and in October one admin key that had sat public since May let an actor publish a third poisoned LiteLLM release. Edge appliances kept falling: Cisco SD-WAN drew an emergency directive in February after Talos traced zero-day exploitation back to 2023, a Palo Alto GlobalProtect bypass ended in Qilin and INC ransomware, credentials for tens of thousands of Fortinet devices leaked, and Citrix, Ivanti and BeyondTrust appliances were exploited as zero-days in the autumn. The year also brought physical effects. Poland's energy sector lost remote terminal units to a wiper in the last days of 2025, Iranian-affiliated actors manipulated internet-exposed PLCs across US critical infrastructure, water utilities went to manual operation, and on the night of 11 to 12 October attacks on supermarket refrigeration controllers at more than 700 French retail sites caused fires and food losses that France attributes to two units of the IRGC's cyber centre. AI moved from forecast into the incident record: an AI agent running an evaluation intruded into Hugging Face's infrastructure in July, Microsoft tied an agentic attack framework to a China-aligned actor's supply-chain compromise of ConnectWise, and two companies said in October that agentic or AI-assisted tooling was used in intrusions against them. The largest theft in this record, about US$6.3 billion from Binance, began with a fake job interview. Governments answered with an emergency directive and a new binding directive that ranks patches by risk, ten joint advisories, the seizure of Chinese state hackers' platforms, the dismantling of botnets, malware-signing and bulletproof services, a national emergency over the US bulk-power system, and arrests of people accused of belonging to ShinyHunters, TeamPCP and KillSec.

This study is the dated record of that year, from 1 January to 11 November 2026; the year is not over, and later events will need their own entries. It is a chronicle, not a deep dive: where another study in this series is the home for an event, this one gives a paragraph and a link. The eMudhra intermediate-certificate compromise is told in [the eMudhra study](/case-studies/EMUDHRA_CA_COMPROMISE_2026.md); the AI agent intrusion into Hugging Face in [the Hugging Face supply chain study](/case-studies/HUGGING_FACE_SUPPLY_CHAIN.md) and [the AI threats study](/case-studies/AI_THREATS_2026.md), which is also the home for the AI distillation advisory; axios, TanStack and Nx in [the npm supply-chain study](/case-studies/NPM_SUPPLY_CHAIN_ATTACKS.md); the critical PHP flaws of October in [the PHP2Shell study](/case-studies/PHP2SHELL_OCTOBER_2026.md); the PLC advisories, the Polish wiper and the refrigeration attacks in [the ICS attacks and advisories study](/case-studies/ICS_ATTACKS_AND_ADVISORIES_2024_2026.md), with Sandworm's part in [the Sandworm study](/case-studies/SANDWORM_OPERATIONS.md); the covert networks and Integrity Technology Group in [the China-attributed actors study](/case-studies/THREAT_ACTORS_CHINA.md); and FSB Center 16's router campaign in [the Russia-attributed actors study](/case-studies/THREAT_ACTORS_RUSSIA.md). The PAN-OS route into Qilin and INC ransomware has its home in [the ransomware ecosystem study](/case-studies/RANSOMWARE_ECOSYSTEM.md), the OpenSSH flaw in [the SSHole study](/case-studies/SSHOLE_CVE_2026_123158.md) and the cloud events in [the cloud security incidents study](/case-studies/CLOUD_SECURITY_INCIDENTS.md). Everything else in 2026 that the verified record supports is here, in date order, with the source behind each line.

### Key findings

- **The tools defenders trust became the way in.** Compromised credentials turned a security scanner's GitHub Actions into credential stealers; LiteLLM believes its compromise came from that scanner in its own CI; PyPI traced the poisoned LiteLLM and telnyx releases to an API token exposed the same way (Confirmed [19][20][21]). A poisoned editor extension reached a GitHub employee and GitHub's internal repositories (Confirmed [36]).
- **One unrevoked secret was enough.** LiteLLM's October post-mortem traces a poisoned release to an admin key committed publicly on 27 May and never revoked; Wiz traced the Qubole intrusion to IAM credentials in a Qubole-associated AWS account that could assume customers' trust roles (Confirmed [117][124]).
- **The edge kept falling, and some exploitation began long before it was disclosed.** Talos traces UAT-8616's Cisco SD-WAN activity back to at least 2023; Volexity saw a zero-day chain against a fully patched Ivanti Connect Secure; ransomware operators used a PAN-OS bypass to defeat MFA on VPN sessions (Confirmed [9][104][47]).
- **Identity and MFA themselves were targets.** AA26-204A and AA26-222A both print T1556.006 Multi-Factor Authentication: LAUNDRY BEAR created application passcodes in Zimbra, and Gunra's actors altered a VDI one-time-password portal so any code they chose succeeded (Confirmed [61][68]).
- **Operational technology was reached through the internet, not through cleverness.** AA26-097A's mitigation is to take PLCs off the public internet; AA26-231A's actors found Siemens S7 PLCs through scanning services and used default or weak credentials; the NCSC warns organisations not to assume OT is unreachable without checking (Confirmed [25][70][75]).
- **Sabotage with physical damage got a state's name on it.** France attributes the October refrigeration attacks, with fires and losses in the tens of millions of euros, to the IRGC-CEC units Shahid Shoostari and Shahid Kaveh, naming the units rather than only the state (Confirmed [159][160]).
- **AI changed speed and cost, not the doors.** The advisories that print T1588.007 still describe default credentials, exposed PLCs and webmail cross-site scripting, and the vendor accounts of agentic intrusions rest on single-vendor telemetry (Confirmed [61][70]; Inferred for the reading).
- **Old bugs still did much of the work.** 88 of the 295 KEV additions of 2026 carry pre-2026 CVE ids, the oldest from 2008; AA26-281A lists a 2015 ProFTPD flaw, told in [the ProFTPD mod_copy study](/case-studies/PROFTPD_MOD_COPY_CVE_2015_3306.md), and AA26-194A a 2018 Cisco Smart Install flaw (Demonstrated [167]; Confirmed [98][60]).

### Top recommendations

1. **Pin what your builds trust and treat every exposure window as a credential leak.** Pin GitHub Actions and packages to verified versions, rotate any credential that a poisoned release could have reached, and move package publishing to trusted publishing, as LiteLLM and telnyx did; Talos advises treating credentials on hosts that installed the bad axios versions as compromised (Confirmed [19][21][24]; Inferred for the general rule).
2. **Revoke secrets the day they leak, and find the ones that already have.** The October LiteLLM compromise started with a key public for four months; Storm-0940's campaign ran through a backup vendor's Microsoft 365 app and its tenants' trust (Confirmed [117][109][120]).
3. **Patch edge appliances on the KEV clock under BOD 26-04's risk tiers, then hunt.** ED 26-03 required inventory and artifact collection within a day and updates within two, then hunting and hardening; BOD 26-04 ranks remediation by exposure, KEV status, automatable exploitation and impact (Confirmed [8][45]).
4. **Take PLCs and other OT off the internet and prove it.** Disconnect PLCs from the public internet and remove inbound port exposure (AA26-097A), change default credentials (AA26-231A), and verify rather than assume that OT is unreachable (NCSC) (Confirmed [25][70][75]).
5. **Protect MFA and the identities that run the cloud.** Watch for new application passcodes and changes to OTP or SSO components, put MFA on every remote access path including VPN, remove never-expiring IAM user keys and protect backup and recovery locks against compromised service principals (Confirmed [61][68][73][90]).
6. **Tune detection and give responders authority.** In AA26-237A the same red team reached full domain compromise at two organizations; the one whose team triaged alerts, cut command and control and isolated hosts contained it, while the other's untuned tools produced noise and no response (Confirmed [73]).

## Scope and method

This study covers intrusions, exploited vulnerabilities, crime, outages and the government response from 1 January to 11 November 2026. It draws on CISA, FBI, NSA and partner advisories, alerts and directives, the FBI's IC3, the Department of Justice, the Treasury, the White House, the US Courts, the UK NCSC and government, France's Ministry for Europe and Foreign Affairs and CERT-FR, CERT Polska (through CISA), CERT-UA, CERT-SE, Singapore's CSA, ENISA's CSIRTs Network, Europol, the Dutch police, the Australian Federal Police, Ireland's Data Protection Commission, the victims' own statements and SEC filings (Stryker, Instructure, DentaQuest, Boston Scientific, IDScan.net, Vercel, ASOS, Railway, Quest Diagnostics, Ahold Delhaize, UNM Health, Vanderbilt University, Cathay Pacific, Malaysia Airlines, ConnectWise, Chainalysis, WorkOS, eMudhra, Hugging Face, OpenAI, LiteLLM, TanStack, Aqua Security, PyPI), the vendors that observed the activity (Mandiant and Google's GTIG, Microsoft, Cisco Talos, Palo Alto Networks and Unit 42, Arctic Wolf, Volexity, ESET, Kaspersky, Sekoia, Wiz, Sansec, Darktrace, Cloudflare, Oracle, Mozilla, Google's Chrome Root Program, Synology, Alpine Linux), the CVE, NVD and KEV records, and the library's own data files. The TeamStarWolf Intelligence Hub feed served as a starting index; its rows are an index, not evidence, and none is cited. Every source was opened on 11 November 2026. Quotations are kept to a few words. Tradecraft is described at the level the advisories use, by technique name and id; no exploit details, commands, payload strings or ransom notes are reproduced.

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|

## Part 2: the edge-device year

**Fortinet, twice.** CVE-2026-24858 let an actor holding any FortiCloud account log in to other users' devices through FortiCloud single sign-on; CISA added it to KEV on 27 January and reported ongoing exploitation the next day (Confirmed [6]). In June, "FortiBleed" put credentials for about 74,000 Fortinet devices in the hands of actors targeting government and private organizations, and in October the FBI and USSS called it a global credential-compromise campaign causing lockouts, citing SOCRadar's count of 86,644 compromised devices in 194 countries (Confirmed [48][49]). The two counts come from different organizations at different times; neither is a census (Inferred). Fortinet had nine KEV additions in 2026 (Demonstrated [167]).

**Cisco SD-WAN and the year's emergency directive.** On 25 February Talos reported that UAT-8616 had exploited CVE-2026-20127, an authentication bypass in the Catalyst SD-WAN Controller, as a zero-day, with activity reaching back to at least 2023 (Confirmed [9]). CISA issued ED 26-03 the same day, requiring federal agencies to inventory their SD-WAN systems and collect artifacts by 26 February, update by 17:00 ET on 27 February, then hunt and harden; the directive was revised on 11 March (Confirmed [7][8]). KEV listed CVE-2026-20127 and the older CVE-2022-20775 on 25 February, CVE-2026-20133 on 20 April and CVE-2026-20182 on 14 May (Confirmed [7]). Talos says UAT-8616 exploited CVE-2026-20182, while other clusters, from March, exploited CVE-2026-20133, CVE-2026-20128 and CVE-2026-20122 with a public proof of concept and deployed web shells (Confirmed [10]). KEV kept listing Catalyst SD-WAN flaws from 25 February to 30 September (Confirmed [167]). [The Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN study](/case-studies/CISCO_SDWAN_CVE_2026_20127.md) is the home for CVE-2026-20127, UAT-8616 and the directive.

**PAN-OS into ransomware.** Palo Alto Networks published CVE-2026-0257, a GlobalProtect authentication bypass, on 13 May (Confirmed [41]). **APT41 and APT31.** Kaspersky attributes the BlackX UEFI bootkit, used against diplomatic agencies in Tajikistan, Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan in early 2026, to APT41 with medium confidence; the group's longer record is in [the APT41 study](/case-studies/APT41_PUBLIC_RECORD.md) (Confirmed [123]). **FSB Center 16 and the router.** AA26-194A, released on 13 July by 17 agencies, says "Russian Federal Security Service (FSB) Center 16 cyber actors" scan for SNMP agents, dump router configurations, exfiltrate over TFTP to VPS infrastructure and sometimes exploit Cisco Smart Install CVE-2018-0171 across critical sectors. The advisory maps 36 ATT&CK ids, including T1027.017 SVG Smuggling for a payload hidden in an SVG element, T1098 and T1556.006 for application passcodes, T1048.003 for exfiltration over DNS and HTTPS, and T1588.007 for AI-assisted tooling (Confirmed [61]). The library has no ATT&CK group object for this actor.

**APT29.** GTIG tracks three suspected Russian espionage clusters abusing app passwords, OAuth, device codes and WhatsApp linking: UNC6293, a moderate-confidence sub-cluster of ICE RELIC (formerly APT29); UNC7005, linked to it with moderate confidence and active February to August 2026; and UNC5976, from March 2026, which may belong to a different service (Confirmed [71]). ESET attributes the Cereal Exchange toolset to Turla with high confidence, used against government organizations in Kazakhstan and Romania to collect NATO and EU correspondence (Confirmed [165]). **APT28.** CERT-UA's #19542 ties UAC-0001 (APT28) to the CVE-2026-21509 lure (Part 3) (Confirmed [5]).

## Part 6: Iran

Iran's actors are traced across years in [the Iran-attributed actors study](/case-studies/THREAT_ACTORS_IRAN.md) and the industrial record in [the ICS attacks and advisories study](/case-studies/ICS_ATTACKS_AND_ADVISORIES_2024_2026.md); this Part dates the 2026 events.

**War context.** US and Israeli strikes on Iran began on 28 February ("Operation Epic Fury"), and Unit 42 counted more than 60 active pro-Iranian hacktivist groups within hours; ESET, citing Symantec and Carbon Black, reports MuddyWater in the networks of several US entities including an airport, a bank and a software firm (Reported [11]).

**Stryker and Handala.** Stryker's 8-K says that on 11 March it identified an incident causing a global disruption to its Microsoft environment and had no indication of ransomware or malware at filing (Confirmed [14]). Its customer update describes shipping delays that forced rescheduling of patient-specific cases in the week of 16 March and thanks the government for seizing the purported actors' domains (Confirmed [18]). Unit 42 tied increased wiper risk to the conflict and said Handala Hack destructive operations reportedly use phishing and administrative access through Microsoft Intune; CISA urged endpoint-management hardening on 18 March; the DOJ on 19 March seized four domains, two of them Handala-branded, used by Iran's MOIS for psychological operations claiming credit for hacks (Confirmed [15][16][17]). ATT&CK lists Handala Hack as an alias of [VOID MANTICORE](/mitre/groups/G1055.md), which it assesses to operate for the MOIS (Demonstrated [171]). ESET, by contrast, calls the group it spells "Hamdala" a pro-Iranian hacktivist group, not state-aligned (Reported [11]). This study carries both labels and does not resolve them.

**PLCs and water.** AA26-097A, first published on 7 April by the FBI, CISA, NSA, EPA, DOE and CNMF and updated on 22 July, says "Iranian-affiliated APT actors" accessed internet-facing PLCs (Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley, with Schneider Electric and Siemens added in the update), extracted and modified project files and altered HMI and SCADA displays across government, water and energy since at least March (Confirmed [25][26]). It calls the activity similar to the November 2023 CyberAv3ngers campaign of the IRGC-CEC but does not attribute it to that group, names no CVEs, and maps ATT&CK for ICS T0883 Internet Accessible Device and T0885 Commonly Used Port with Enterprise T1219, T1041 and T1565 (Confirmed [25]). Its mitigation is to disconnect PLCs from the public internet and remove inbound port exposure (Confirmed [25]). On 30 July CISA reported a significant increase in PLC targeting in water and wastewater, with operators locked out by password changes and PLCs disconnected by IP changes, leading to boil-water notices and manual operations; the alert names no actor (Confirmed [63]).

**ZionSiphon.** Darktrace analysed ZionSiphon, OT malware aimed at Israeli water treatment and desalination with sabotage logic for chlorine and pressure controls; Risky Business reports that it failed because it was buggy (Confirmed [27]; Reported [28]). No source opened here attributes it.

**CHOSEN BRICK.** On 15 September the NCSC, with the FBI and the Netherlands' AIVD, exposed Iranian state spear-phishing and CHOSEN BRICK, Windows spyware used against dissidents, activists and journalists, including in the UK; no specific Iranian body is named (Confirmed [86]).

**The refrigeration attacks.** [The European refrigeration attacks study](/case-studies/EUROPEAN_REFRIGERATION_ATTACKS_2026.md) is the home for these attacks; this is the summary. On the night of 11 to 12 October attacks on supermarket refrigeration controllers at more than 700 French retail sites caused material damage, fires and food losses of tens of millions of euros (Confirmed [159]). ## Part 15: the defender view

**Pin the pipeline and assume the exposure window leaked.** Pin actions and packages to verified versions, rotate credentials on every host that ran a poisoned release, publish through trusted publishing, and revoke a committed secret the day it is found (Confirmed [19][21][24][117]; Inferred for the general rule). See [Supply Chain Security](/SUPPLY_CHAIN_SECURITY.md).

**Patch the edge on the risk clock, then hunt.** ED 26-03's sequence (inventory, artifacts, update, hunt, harden) is the model for any appliance zero-day, and BOD 26-04 ranks remediation by exposure, KEV status, automatable exploitation and impact (Confirmed [8][45]). AA26-194A's network-device asks are to disable Smart Install, use SNMPv3 with authentication and privacy, drop SNMPv1 and v2 and replace end-of-life devices (Confirmed [60]).

**Take OT off the internet.** Disconnect PLCs from the public internet and remove inbound port exposure, follow secure connectivity principles for OT, change default credentials and verify reachability rather than assume it (Confirmed [25][70][75]).

**Defend MFA itself.** Both AA26-204A and AA26-222A show MFA components altered or bypassed; watch for new application passcodes, changes to OTP portals and SSO computer accounts, and require MFA and account lockout on VPN and webmail (Confirmed [61][68][73]).

**Govern cloud identities like administrators.** Remove never-expiring IAM user keys, scope service principals, protect backup and recovery locks, and treat cross-account trust roles as credentials a vendor can lose (Confirmed [73][90][124]).

**Harden endpoint management.** After the Stryker attack CISA urged hardening of endpoint-management systems; an attacker with administrative access to Intune holds a software-deployment channel to every managed device (Confirmed [16][15]; Inferred for the reading).

**Tune the SOC and let it act.** AA26-237A's two outcomes were decided by alert tuning and by responders able to cut command and control and isolate hosts (Confirmed [73]).

**Hunting cues from the record.** At the advisory level, and without commands. Each cue is this study's reading of a behavior the sources describe (behaviors Confirmed; cues Inferred):

- Build systems: tags on third-party actions that move, workflows that read runner memory or caches across forks, and publish tokens used from new places (Confirmed [19][34]).
- Packages: install-time scripts that fetch further payloads, and new Python startup hooks in environments that installed a poisoned release (Confirmed [23] for install-time scripts; behavior per Microsoft's title [118]; cue Inferred).
- Edge: new administrative sessions from FortiCloud SSO, SD-WAN controller peering changes, web shells on NetScaler and PeopleSoft hosts, and MFA-less VPN sessions after a GlobalProtect login (Confirmed [6][10][92][44][47]).
- Identity: new application passcodes in webmail, edits to OTP portal files, OAuth tokens used from unfamiliar ranges, and service principals issuing bulk deletes (Confirmed [61][68][50][90]).
- OT: project-file uploads and downloads on PLCs, HMI display changes, PLC credential and IP changes, and S7 data-block writes from new hosts (Confirmed [25][63][70]).
- Network devices: SNMP queries from unexpected sources and configuration transfers over TFTP (Confirmed [60]).

**Exposure management.** See the library's [Vulnerability Prioritization Reference](/VULNERABILITY_PRIORITIZATION_REFERENCE.md), [Edge Device Security Reference](/EDGE_DEVICE_SECURITY_REFERENCE.md), [Identity Security Reference](/IDENTITY_SECURITY_REFERENCE.md), [Cloud Attack Reference](/CLOUD_ATTACK_REFERENCE.md), [ICS/OT Security Reference](/ICS_OT_SECURITY_REFERENCE.md) and [Ransomware Defense Reference](/RANSOMWARE_DEFENSE_REFERENCE.md).

## Cross-cutting themes

- **The shared tool was the attack surface (Inferred).** A vulnerability scanner, an AI gateway, an HTTP client, a family of JavaScript packages (TanStack), an editor extension, a backup vendor's Microsoft 365 app and an SEO tracker each carried an intrusion to their users in 2026 (Confirmed [19][20][23][34][36][109][152]). Supply-chain compromises chained within 12 days in March: Trivy on 19 March, LiteLLM through its Trivy CI dependency on 24 March, then axios on 31 March (Confirmed [19][20][23]). See [Supply Chain Security](/SUPPLY_CHAIN_SECURITY.md).
- **The edge stayed the front door (Inferred).** Cisco SD-WAN, Citrix NetScaler, FortiGate and SonicWall in the two #StopRansomware advisories, PAN-OS, Ivanti, BeyondTrust, and SOHO and enterprise routers in AA26-113A and AA26-194A were the year's ways in, and Cisco is the second-largest vendor among 2026 KEV additions (Confirmed [7][91][68][113][47][104][106][29][60]; Demonstrated [167]). See [Edge Device Security](/EDGE_DEVICE_SECURITY_REFERENCE.md).
- **Identity over malware (Inferred).** Valid and default accounts, MFA tampering (T1556.006 in both AA26-204A and AA26-222A), stolen session cookies, OAuth tokens, application passcodes and cloud service principals recur across the year's records (Confirmed [61][68][50][90][73]). See [Identity Security](/IDENTITY_SECURITY_REFERENCE.md).
- **AI showed up on both sides (Inferred).** T1588.007 is printed by AA26-204A and AA26-231A, AA26-251A is mapped wholly to ATLAS, and vendors and victims describe agentic frameworks in intrusions (Confirmed [61][70][83][120][139][140]). See [AI Security](/AI_SECURITY_REFERENCE.md).
- **OT exposure had physical consequences (Inferred).** Damaged RTUs, PLCs on manual operation, fires in supermarkets and a blood bank's temperature alarms are in the 2026 record (Confirmed [1][63][159]; Reported [153]). See [ICS/OT Security](/ICS_OT_SECURITY_REFERENCE.md).
- **Enforcement went after the plumbing and the people (Inferred).** Signing services, botnets, bulletproof hosts, scanning platforms and laundering networks were seized or sanctioned, and the year brought arrests tied to the groups behind its loudest extortion and supply-chain campaigns (Confirmed [37][40][52][76][146][51][74]; Reported [130][93][94]). See [Ransomware Defense](/RANSOMWARE_DEFENSE_REFERENCE.md) and [Malware Families](/MALWARE_FAMILIES.md).

### What is overstated and what is understated

- **Overstated: AI as a new attack class.** The 2026 advisories that print T1588.007 still describe old entry points: default credentials, internet-exposed PLCs and cross-site scripting in webmail. AI changed speed and cost, not the doors, and the "agentic" attack claims rest on single-vendor telemetry; the strongest primary, Hugging Face's, concerns an evaluation agent, not a criminal operator (Confirmed [61][70][57]; Reported [90][84]; Inferred for the reading).
- **Overstated: firm national labels.** The sources' own labels are weaker than a firm state attribution: "China-state aligned" (Storm-0940), "Iranian-affiliated" (AA26-097A) and "China-nexus" (AA26-113A); AA26-097A explicitly does not attribute the PLC activity to CyberAv3ngers, AA26-231A and the NCSC's OT alert name no state, and AA26-251A names commercial companies with only "Likely" government awareness (Confirmed [120][25][29][70][75][83]).
- **Overstated: the early Stryker "wiper" story.** Stryker's own 8-K reported no indication of ransomware or malware at filing; the wiper and Intune account come from Unit 42's "reportedly" and from ESET, which calls the group a hacktivist one (Confirmed [14][15]; Reported [11]).
- **Overstated: dollar figures in early crypto-theft reporting.** Bitget revised its figure after first reports; Binance's loss is US$6.3 billion in one outlet and US$6.43 billion in another, with frozen amounts of about US$3 billion and US$2.85 billion (Reported [89][135][137]).
- **Overstated: the precision of the advisories' ATT&CK tables.** AA26-293A files UAC-bypass text under T1059 and Mimikatz credential dumping under T1560.002 Archive via Library, and AA26-281A maps to v19; ids should be checked, not copied (Confirmed [113][98]; Inferred for the reading).

- **AA26-231A's T1694.** The advisory lists T1694 Insecure Credentials under Lateral Movement in a mixed Enterprise and ICS table; the library carries T1694 only in its ATT&CK for ICS data, with Persistence and Lateral Movement tactics, so it is bound as an ICS technique (Confirmed [70]; Demonstrated [174]).
