## Executive summary

This is a story about how fast an open-source community can move when it has to. On 4 November 2026 the OpenSSH maintainers learned that, on some Linux systems, sshd would accept a password login it should have refused (**Confirmed** [2]). The fix commit was authored at 21:18 UTC the same day (**Confirmed** [4]). By the next day OpenSSH had shipped Portable 10.6p2, Alpine Linux had published fixed packages for every branch back to 3.16 and four point releases, the musl maintainer had posted his own advisory, and the disclosure was on oss-security (**Confirmed** [1] [13] [14] [7] [6]). Gentoo, Void Linux, OpenWrt, Buildroot and OpenEmbedded picked up the fix within about a day (**Confirmed** [20] [21] [22] [24] [25]). The flaw was reported by Ozgun Kultekin of Wakefield (**Confirmed** [1]); the oss-security subject line spells it "sshole", the researcher's site is sshhole.com, and the press wrote SSHole (**Confirmed** [6]; **Reported** [40] [47]).

The flaw is narrow. It affects OpenSSH on Linux built against the musl C library and run without PAM, with password authentication on, and only for an account whose password field is a single asterisk while another local account holds a SHA-256 or SHA-512 crypt hash (**Confirmed** [13]; the musl and PAM scope also [7] [10]). Alpine Linux is the named example (**Confirmed** [10]). Red Hat and Ubuntu, whose builds use glibc and PAM, and SUSE say their products are not affected (**Confirmed** [15] [18] [19]). Where the conditions hold, though, an attacker who can reach the SSH port and knows an eligible account name logs in without a password, in one connection, reliably (**Confirmed** [13]).

The MITRE CVE Numbering Authority published CVE-2026-123158 at 15:46 UTC on 5 November, scored 8.1 High with CWE-394 (**Confirmed** [10]). Honeypot operators reported exploitation attempts within hours of the CVE (**Reported** [43] [42]) Previdian told Help Net Security on 6 November that it had root access on an Alpine honeypot (**Reported** [44]), and GreyNoise confirmed its first successful exploitation of one of its own sensors at 00:46 UTC on 7 November (**Reported** [41]). CISA added the CVE to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 9 November with a three-day deadline under Binding Operational Directive 26-04 and a forensic triage requirement (**Confirmed** [34] [35]). It is the only OpenSSH CVE in KEV (**Confirmed** [35]).

This study sets out the technical record at advisory level, a dated timeline from the fix to the KEV deadline, what the sensor networks saw and what they did not, the exposure picture across distributions, cloud images, containers and embedded builds, the vulnerability-management lessons, the defender playbook, the lineage of earlier OpenSSH flaws it belongs to, and the MITRE ATT&CK, CWE and D3FEND bindings with a Navigator layer.

### Key findings

- **The fix came before the CVE.** The fix commit was authored at 21:18 UTC on 4 November, the day OpenSSH learned of the bug, and 10.6p2 shipped on 5 November; the CVE record followed at 15:46 UTC that day and the 10.6p2 release notes carry no CVE id (**Confirmed** [4] [2] [1] [10]). OpenSSH said it released quickly because AI agents had rediscovered bugs within hours, and that it now regards long embargoes as mainly benefiting attackers (**Confirmed** [2]).
- **Three parties, three readings of the root cause.** OpenSSH says the root cause is outside sshd but exploitable and avoidable there (**Confirmed** [1]). The oss-security disclosure describes the mechanism: for an account whose password field is `*`, a long password makes musl's `crypt(3)` return `*` (**Confirmed** [6]). The musl maintainer calls it erroneous use by the caller, not a musl bug, and expects musl's error mechanism to change (**Confirmed** [7] [8]). Alpine's summary explains the collision: musl's `crypt()` returns `*` as an error indicator for an over-long key, and that matches a passwordless account's `*` field (**Confirmed** [13]).
- **The conditions are specific, and common in one ecosystem.** All four must hold: sshd without PAM, `PasswordAuthentication yes` (the default), an account whose password field is `*`, and a `$5$` or `$6$` hash on some other local account (**Confirmed** [13]). Alpine ships root as `root:*` since about 3.16, and its tiny-cloud images from 3.18.11 to 3.24.3 ship an `alpine` user with a `*` field and passwordless doas (**Confirmed** [13]).
- **Attempts came within hours; confirmed compromise came later and only on sensors.** Previdian saw a first attempt about 8 hours after CVE publication and told Help Net Security on 6 November of root access on an Alpine honeypot; SANS ISC saw attempts on 5 November; GreyNoise's first confirmed successful chain came about 32 hours after NVD publication (**Reported** [43] [44] [42] [41]; the comparison is Inferred).
- **Scan volume is not compromise.** GreyNoise counted 33.7 million attempted SSH sessions on its sensors from 5 to 11 November, 5 possible exploitation attempts and 26 confirmed post-exploitation chains from 8 attacker addresses (**Reported** [41]).
- **The trackers disagree, in both directions.** Red Hat shows a preliminary 9.8 and CWE-303 while saying its products are not affected; Debian marks glibc releases vulnerable at urgency "unimportant"; Ubuntu rates the CVE High solely because it is in KEV while listing every release Not affected (**Confirmed** [15] [17] [18]). NVD's CPE configuration names only Alpine (**Confirmed** [11]).
- **The hard-to-see population is embedded.** Medcrypt reports reproducing the flaw on a stock Yocto image and calls musl without PAM, with password authentication and `*`-locked service accounts, a common hardened embedded baseline (**Reported** [9]). No vendor advisory or CPE entry covers those builds (Inferred from [11]).
- **Patching the package is not enough.** Upgrading the package does not replace the running daemon; sshd must be restarted (**Confirmed** [13]).

### Top recommendations

1. On every Linux host that runs OpenSSH on musl (Alpine, Yocto, Buildroot, OpenWrt with the openssh package, Alpine-based containers that run sshd), upgrade to 10.6p2 or the patched distribution package, then restart sshd [1] [13].
2. Where that cannot happen today, set `PasswordAuthentication no`, after confirming key-based login works and while keeping an existing session open, then restart sshd [13] [7].
3. Decide exposure by the four Alpine conditions and the libc, not by the OpenSSH version string: Alpine backported the fix into older version strings, and glibc and PAM hosts flagged by version are not exploitable [13] [15] [18].
4. For internet-facing hosts, treat the KEV due date of 12 November 2026 as the deadline and perform forensic triage, as the KEV entry requires [34] [37].
5. Hunt for sshd "Accepted password" log lines for accounts that have no password, review doas use, new users, changed files and authorized keys, and treat a host as compromised if a login cannot be ruled out [13].
6. Rotate any credentials and SSH keys a compromised host could read, and remove attacker-added keys and accounts before returning it to service [41] [13] (Inferred from the reported post-exploitation).

## Part 1: The technical record

### What OpenSSH says

OpenSSH Portable 10.6p2 was released on 5 November 2026. Its notes say it contains a critical security workaround for Linux systems that use musl libc with PAM disabled (**Confirmed** [1]). The single Security entry says sshd now avoids a password authentication bypass that affects some Linux systems built and run without PAM, and that the project considers the root cause to lie outside sshd, though it is exploitable through sshd and avoidable there (**Confirmed** [1]). The previous release, 10.6p1, had shipped on 6 October 2026 (**Confirmed** [1]).

Damien Miller's mail to openssh-unix-dev, "urgent security release: 10.6p2", told operators of musl Linux systems without PAM to set `PasswordAuthentication=no` or disable SSH immediately, and said the bug does not exist on non-Linux systems, on Linux with glibc, or on Linux systems that use PAM for password authentication (**Confirmed** [2]). He posted the same announcement on Mastodon at 09:03:46 UTC on 5 November (**Confirmed** [3]).

### The fix

The fix is openssh-portable commit `4353fdc43ef646c43a8dda7025cc1a898caf1482`, "avoid password authn bypass", by Damien Miller, authored at 21:18 UTC and committed at 21:57 UTC on 4 November 2026 (**Confirmed** [4]). It changes `auth-passwd.c`, `openbsd-compat/port-uw.c` and `openbsd-compat/xcrypt.c`, and its message says the root cause is not within OpenSSH (**Confirmed** [4]). Password checking now works on private copies of the stored hash and rejects a match when the stored hash is shorter than a minimum length (**Confirmed** [4]); the oss-security disclosure describes the same change as a check for a minimum valid hash length (**Confirmed** [6]).

### Why it happens, at advisory level

Alpine's root-cause summary is the clearest. Without PAM, sshd compares the `crypt()` of the login attempt with the stored password field, and when the stored field is too short to carry a salt it borrows another account's salt. musl's `crypt()` returns `*` as an error indicator for an over-long key, and that string matches the `*` field of a passwordless account (**Confirmed** [13]). The CVE record puts the same thing in weakness terms: the bypass relates to misreading whether a string is a password hash, and for some account configurations an over-long password succeeds (**Confirmed** [10]). The musl maintainer adds that musl's failure token, `*`, is the conventional shadow "no password" value, and proposes changing musl's tokens to `*0` and `*1` (**Confirmed** [8]).

The researcher's write-up says he found the bug about four days after an OpenSSH commit of 30 October 2026 that touched `pick_salt()`, that it ran about 17,600 test logins in which only musl without PAM accepted the invalid passwords, and that the bug has existed since 2016 with the musl behavior dating to 2012 (**Reported** [40]). The researcher, The Hacker News and Help Net Security trace SSHole to the fake-salt handling OpenSSH added in 2016 for CVE-2016-6210 (**Reported** [40] [45] [44]; the 2016 fix itself is **Confirmed** [1], see Part 7).

### Who says the root cause is theirs

| Party | Position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| OpenSSH | Root cause outside sshd; exploitable through sshd and avoidable there; fixed by a workaround in sshd | [1] [4] (Confirmed) |
| oss-security disclosure (Jan Schaumann) | Describes the mechanism (musl `crypt(3)` returning `*`) and quotes OpenSSH on the root cause; does not assign it; fix checks for a minimum valid hash length; mitigation is `PasswordAuthentication no` | [6] (Confirmed) |
| musl (Rich Felker) | Erroneous use by the caller, not a musl bug; the crypt error mechanism will likely change in the next release cycle | [7] [8] (Confirmed) |
| Red Hat | CWE-303, Incorrect Implementation of Authentication Algorithm | [15] (Confirmed) |
| CVE record (MITRE CNA) | CWE-394, Unexpected Status Code or Return Value | [10] (Confirmed) |

All of these can be true at once: a C library returned a legitimate but unexpected value, and a caller treated it as a hash (Inferred).

### The conditions, as Alpine states them

Alpine says a host is affected only when all of the following hold (**Confirmed** [13]):

1. It runs `openssh-server` built without PAM, or `openssh-server-pam` with `UsePAM no`.
2. `PasswordAuthentication` is `yes`, which is the default.
3. An account has `*` as its password field.
4. At least one local account has a `$5$` or `$6$` password hash.

Alpine lists the accounts that carry `*`: root (shipped as `root:*` since about 3.16 and protected by the default `PermitRootLogin prohibit-password`), key-only users, directory-service users through musl-nscd, and the tiny-cloud `alpine` user (**Confirmed** [13]). It lists as not affected: containers that do not run sshd, `PasswordAuthentication no`, `openssh-server-pam` with `UsePAM yes`, accounts locked with `!`, hosts with no `$`-style hash, and cloud-init images, because cloud-init writes `PasswordAuthentication no` (**Confirmed** [13]).

### What an attacker gets

Alpine says an attacker who can reach the SSH port and knows an eligible account name can log in without a password, in one connection and reliably (**Confirmed** [13]). Access carries the compromised account's permissions (**Reported** [45]). Root is not reached directly under the default `PermitRootLogin prohibit-password` (**Confirmed** [13]). The tiny-cloud images are the exception that matters: their `alpine` user has passwordless doas, so the images are safe on first boot but become exploitable, with a path to root, once any `$6$` hash exists on the machine (**Confirmed** [13]). The researcher says the same of Alpine's official AWS and Azure images (**Reported** [40]).

### Disagreements in the record

- **One connection or two.** Alpine says one connection, reliably (**Confirmed** [13]). Medcrypt's oss-security post mentions a two-attempt requirement (**Reported** [9]). The study does not resolve this; both are printed.
- **CVSS vector.** The CNA scores attack complexity High (8.1); Red Hat's draft scores it Low (9.8) (**Confirmed** [10] [15]). The four preconditions above argue for the CNA's AC:H reading on most hosts, and for Red Hat's on a tiny-cloud instance that already holds a `$6$` hash (Inferred).
- **Name.** The oss-security subject says `sshole`, Gentoo's bug title `*sshole`, the researcher's site sshhole.com, and the press SSHole (**Confirmed** [6] [20]; **Reported** [40] [47]).
- **When the public exploit appeared.** GreyNoise's timeline puts the first public exploit on GitHub at 13:16:55 UTC on 5 November, about four hours after the fix went public and about 2.5 hours before the CVE id was reserved (**Reported** [41]). Previdian's timeline puts a public proof of concept at 17:30 UTC (**Reported** [43]).

## Part 3: Who exploited it

### What the government record says

CISA's alert of 9 November added four KEV entries "based on evidence of active exploitation", among them CVE-2026-123158 (**Confirmed** [36]). ### What attackers did after login

GreyNoise reports, at technique level (**Reported** [41]):

- host, network, process and SSH-configuration discovery, and `sshd-session` spawning `sh`;
- cloud instance metadata queries;
- repeated attempts, from 8 to 10 November, to add attacker SSH keys to the root and `alpine` authorized keys, including one key replacement on `alpine` on 8 November;
- doas attempts to read the shadow file from 7 November;
- creation of a local account with a doas rule for passwordless root on 10 November, followed by removal of its home directory;
- name-resolution (DNS configuration) changes, a backdoor SSH server, a startup service for persistence, and a Python agent polling an HTTPS command-and-control server.

BleepingComputer, citing GreyNoise, adds attempts to read password hashes and root SSH keys (**Reported** [46]). The Hacker News reports discovery commands over non-interactive sessions (**Reported** [45]).

### The GT10 cluster and the TAC Agent

GreyNoise tracks a "major" activity cluster it calls GT10: two intrusions and seven addresses, some of them possibly compromised infrastructure, on 7 and 8 November (**Reported** [41]). It says not all SSHole activity is GT10 and names no actor or country (**Reported** [41]). GT10's intrusions ran discovery and network tests, staged tools, changed name resolution, started a backdoor SSH server and dropped a Python runtime with a "TAC Agent": a standard-library Python 3 implant, version 0.1.1, with a shared hard-coded enrollment token, a beacon of about 150 seconds and unsigned self-update, whose enrollment never succeeded on the first victim (**Reported** [41]). The agent's comments and logs are in Chinese (**Reported** [41]). GreyNoise flags its TAC Agent analysis as LLM-generated and some GT10 evidence as medium confidence (**Reported** [41]). BleepingComputer reported that the Chinese-language comments may aid attribution alongside other evidence (**Reported** [46]).

### Attribution: none

No opened primary names a threat actor or attributes the activity to a country. GT10 is an activity cluster, and Chinese-language strings in a tool are an artifact, not attribution (Inferred [41]). watchTowr's Jake Knott said that so far only botnets and crypto miners had adopted the flaw, and Defused saw a small number of actors, not widespread activity (**Reported** [48] [46]); both firms' original posts could not be opened. No ATT&CK group, software or campaign object applies (Inferred; see Part 8).

## Part 5: The vulnerability-management view

### Where the advisory lived

The OpenSSH security page, as opened on 11 November, had no entry newer than 9 April 2025 (**Confirmed** [5]). The bypass appears only in the release notes, so a team that watches only the security page would have missed it (Inferred from [5] [1]). The 10.6p2 notes also say the team is receiving many AI-assisted bug reports, has seen AI-found bugs rediscovered independently, and will make more frequent releases instead of batching fixes (**Confirmed** [1]). Feeds that key on the release notes, the CVE record and KEV will see these releases sooner (Inferred).

### False positives and false negatives

- **False positives from version matching.** The CPE range is "OpenSSH before 10.6p2" with a platform condition, so scanners that match on version alone will flag Debian, Ubuntu and RHEL hosts that cannot be exploited; triage has to key on the libc, PAM use and the `PasswordAuthentication` setting (Inferred from [10] [11] [15] [18]). Debian's "vulnerable" rows at urgency "unimportant" and Ubuntu's High priority on a Not affected package are the same signal seen from the distribution side (**Confirmed** [17] [18]).
- **False negatives from the CPE set.** NVD's CPE configuration names only Alpine, so CPE-driven scanners will not flag Yocto, Buildroot or appliance builds of musl sshd without PAM; embedded fleets depend on device makers shipping 10.6p2 or a backport (Inferred from [11] [9]).
- **Backported versions.** Alpine's fixed packages for older branches keep their older version numbers (for example 3.24's 10.3_p1-r2), so a version-only rule also misses fixed hosts in the other direction (Inferred from [13]).
- **A same-week errata that is not the fix.** RHSA-2026:97866 (Moderate, RHEL 9, 10 November) fixes CVE-2026-59997, CVE-2026-59998 and CVE-2026-60000, not CVE-2026-123158, and must not be read as an SSHole response (**Confirmed** [16]).
- **Scanner support.** google/goonami-scanner added the CVE id to its existing `opensshauthbypass` detector on 5 November (**Confirmed** [33]).

### KEV, BOD 26-04 and the three-day clock

BOD 26-04, issued 10 June 2026, supersedes BOD 19-02 and BOD 22-01 and sets remediation urgency from four inputs: public exposure, KEV status, whether an adversary can automate exploitation, and technical impact (**Confirmed** [37]). Its Table 1 gives 3 days plus forensic triage for an exposed, KEV-listed, not-automatable, total-control vulnerability (row 3), and 14 days when the system is not exposed (row 11) (**Confirmed** [37]). Removing a system from the internet flips the exposure input and pushes the timeline back (**Confirmed** [37]).

Applied to the CISA ADP values for SSHole, the table gives 3 days plus forensic triage for internet-facing hosts, which matches the KEV due date of 12 November, and 14 days for internal-only hosts (Inferred from [37] [10] [34]). A program running a 30-day KEV cycle would leave an internet-facing host open about 27 days past the federal deadline and about a month (32 days) after GreyNoise's first observed successful exploitation (Inferred). The KEV entry requires forensic triage and notes the flaw sits in an open-source component other products may use (**Confirmed** [34]).

### Waiting for KEV starts late

The patch was public four days before the KEV listing, and sensor telemetry of exploitation came two to four days before it (Inferred from [1] [34] [41] [42] [43]). As opened on 11 November, the CISA ADP SSVC on the CVE record reads Exploitation active with a date-only 5 November timestamp, but the container was last updated on 10 November and cites the 8 November SANS diary and the 9 November KEV entry, so when "active" was first set is not established (**Confirmed** [10]). A queue triggered by the vendor advisory on 5 November would have started four days earlier than one triggered by KEV (Inferred). The TeamStarWolf Intelligence Hub feed carries the KEV row for the CVE dated 9 November and headline rows from 5 November onward (**Confirmed** [57]).

## Part 6: The defender view

### Fix

1. Upgrade to 10.6p2 or the patched distribution package (the Alpine package versions are in Part 4) (**Confirmed** [1] [13]).
2. Restart sshd. Upgrading the package does not replace the running daemon (**Confirmed** [13]).
3. Rebuilt images do not fix running instances: update tiny-cloud instances in place (**Confirmed** [13]), and treat long-lived containers that run sshd the same way (Inferred).

### Bridge until the fix lands

- Set `PasswordAuthentication no` and restart sshd, after confirming that public-key login works and while keeping an existing session open; verify the effective configuration (**Confirmed** [13] [7]). Either updating or disabling password logins closes the hole (**Confirmed** [13]).
- Keep the default `PermitRootLogin prohibit-password`. It keeps a `root:*` account out of reach, but it does not help when another reachable account can escalate, for example through doas (**Confirmed** [13]).
- Lock accounts that should never log in with `!` rather than `*` (Inferred from Alpine's not-affected list [13]).
- Restrict SSH exposure (remove it from the internet, use a jump host, allow-list source ranges). This changes the BOD 26-04 exposure input and limits attackers to internal sources, but the flaw stays exploitable from any host that can reach port 22 (Inferred from [37]); none of the opened primaries names jump hosts specifically.

### Detect

- **Logs.** Look for sshd "Accepted password" lines for accounts that have no password (`alpine`, root, key-only users) (**Confirmed** [13]).
- **Host review.** Review doas use, recently created users, recently changed files and authorized keys; if a login cannot be ruled out, treat the host as compromised (**Confirmed** [13]).
- **Persistence artifacts.** Look for unauthorized SSH keys, the TAC Agent and its Python runtime, and unexpected startup services, the artifacts GreyNoise reports attackers left (Inferred from [41] [46]).
- **Session fingerprints.** GreyNoise publishes HASSH client fingerprints, notes a Paramiko-tagged exploit client, and flags `sshd-session` spawning `sh` (**Reported** [41]).
- **Blocking.** One command-and-control address GreyNoise saw is shared Cloudflare space, so block the domain rather than the address; some attacking addresses may be compromised legitimate infrastructure (**Reported** [41]).

### Telling an attempt from a compromise

A long password in a log is not proof of SSHole. SANS saw long-password entries on 1 November, before disclosure, from buggy brute-force scripts pasting shell commands into the password field, and its Cowrie honeypots keep only the first 255 characters, so password-length alerts on such sensors cannot by themselves separate SSHole attempts from other junk (**Reported** [42]). Because SSH is encrypted, GreyNoise says exploitation is hard to confirm from traffic, and its confirmed counts rest on observed shell commands (**Reported** [41]). The reliable signal is on the host: a successful password login for an account that has no password (**Confirmed** [13]).

### Triage after a suspected login

The KEV entry requires forensic triage under CISA's requirements (**Confirmed** [34]). Given what GreyNoise reports attackers did after login, a triage should cover authorized keys for every account (root and `alpine` in particular), new local accounts and doas rules, startup services, DNS configuration, new SSH servers or listeners, Python runtimes and agents, outbound HTTPS beacons, cloud metadata access, and any credential or private key the host could read (Inferred from [41] [46]). Rotate those credentials and keys, and rebuild where a login cannot be ruled out (Inferred from [13]).

## Cross-cutting themes

- **The embargo is shrinking.** OpenSSH fixed and shipped within about a day of learning of the bug and said AI agents had rediscovered bugs within hours (**Confirmed** [2] [4]). A public exploit appeared before the CVE id existed (**Reported** [41]). Programs whose intake starts at a CVE id or a KEV listing start late; intake has to watch release notes and mailing lists too (Inferred). See [the AI Threats 2026 case study](/case-studies/AI_THREATS_2026.md) and [the CVE-2026-98432 case study](/case-studies/LINUX_TCP_UAF_2026.md) for the same pressure from the exploit side.
- **The interface between libraries is a security boundary.** A C library returned an error token; a caller compared it as a hash; a shadow-file convention made the two collide (**Confirmed** [13] [7] [8]). Each party can say the root cause is elsewhere; OpenSSH and Alpine shipped changes, and musl plans one (**Confirmed** [1] [13] [7]; Inferred for the reading).
- **The same CVE means different things on different hosts.** A Critical 9.8 on a platform that is not affected, a "vulnerable" row at "unimportant" urgency, and a High priority on a Not affected package all describe the same CVE (**Confirmed** [15] [17] [18]). Context (libc, PAM, configuration, exposure) changes the risk more than the score does, which is the argument of [the Vulnerability Prioritization Reference](/VULNERABILITY_PRIORITIZATION_REFERENCE.md) (Inferred).
- **Defaults decide exposure.** Password authentication on by default, root shipped as `*`, a convenience user with passwordless doas, and cloud-init writing a safer default all moved hosts into or out of scope (**Confirmed** [13]). See [the Linux Hardening Reference](/LINUX_HARDENING_REFERENCE.md).
- **Embedded is where the long tail lives.** Distribution packages shipped within a day; Yocto, Buildroot and appliance builds patch on vendor firmware cycles and sit outside the CPE set (**Confirmed** [24] [25] [11]; Inferred for the tail). See [the Firmware and IoT Security Reference](/FIRMWARE_IOT_SECURITY_REFERENCE.md).
- **Patch the daemon, not just the package.** Alpine's restart warning applies to every long-running service (**Confirmed** [13]).

### What is overstated and what is understated

- **Overstated: exploitation within hours.** Exploitation attempts appeared about 8 hours after CVE publication (Previdian) and on 5 November (SANS), and Previdian reported root on one Alpine honeypot on 6 November, but GreyNoise's first confirmed successful chain came about 32 hours after NVD publication, and every success reported is on a sensor or honeypot, not a named victim (**Reported** [43] [44] [42] [41]; Inferred for the comparison). Coverage that runs attempts and compromise together overstates both speed and reach.
- **Overstated: scan volume as attacks.** The 27.5 million to 45.5 million port-22 figures are general SSH background, not SSHole exploitation; GreyNoise itself counts 5 possible exploitation attempts among 33.7 million sessions (**Reported** [41] [46]; Inferred for the reading). Every confirmed chain is on a sensor network, not a named victim (Inferred [41]).
- **Overstated: all of OpenSSH on Linux, and root on any Alpine box.** Red Hat, Ubuntu and SUSE say their glibc and PAM builds are not affected, and the CVE is scoped to musl without PAM (**Confirmed** [15] [18] [19] [10]). Alpine root is protected by the default `PermitRootLogin prohibit-password`; root comes through the tiny-cloud user's passwordless doas or a changed setting (**Confirmed** [13]).
- **Overstated: tracker severity.** Red Hat's 9.8 on a platform it calls not affected, and version-only scanner hits on glibc hosts, inflate the scope (**Confirmed** [15]; Inferred for the scanner effect).
- **Overstated: attribution.** Chinese-language comments in one cluster's agent are an artifact, not attribution; no primary names an actor (Inferred [41]).
- **Understated: embedded and appliance fleets.** Medcrypt's Yocto reproduction and the "hardened" embedded baseline sit outside NVD's CPE set and outside distribution advisories (**Reported** [9]; Inferred from [11]).
- **Understated: running cloud instances.** Tiny-cloud instances built from 3.18.11 to 3.24.3 stay exploitable until each is updated, and rebuilding the image does not fix them (**Confirmed** [13]).
- **Understated: persistence after patching.** CISA requires forensic triage, and GreyNoise saw authorized keys and new local accounts within days; a host patched after exposure can still carry a planted key (**Confirmed** [34]; **Reported** [41]).
- **Understated: the KEV clock.** A three-day window is short, and it began four days after the patch (**Confirmed** [34] [1]; Inferred for the reading).
