## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY | An adaptable service, not a malicious file plus a cure

- GandCrab is a case about a criminal service and a sustained recovery effort, not a single executable followed by a universal fix.
- Its affiliate model separated development from distribution, so the same family could follow very different initial access.
- The study separates a reported operator shutdown from elimination of criminal capability, and a working decryptor from full recovery.
- It does not claim every victim recovered every file, or that all later activity belongs to the same people.

## PART 1 | The affiliate model separated development from distribution

- Bitdefender described developers retaining a share of affiliate proceeds while affiliates handled infection and extortion. [5]
- Distribution examples included spam, exploit-kit delivery and remote access; these describe a service ecosystem, not one person.
- A defender may meet the same family after very different initial events, so the payload can look familiar while the access path differs.
- Keep the affiliate and RaaS structure distinct from a single named actor; continuity needs its own evidence, not a similar look.

## PART 1 | Version specificity decided whether files were recoverable

- Support changed over time and did not simply cover every numbered release; the early page's update excludes versions 2 and 3. [1]
- "A GandCrab decryptor exists" is therefore insufficient to decide whether a particular set of files can be recovered.
- A real assessment needs the variant identification, preserved encrypted files and the tool's documented applicability.
- A filename extension or ransom note may help, but should not be treated as infallible proof of the exact variant.

## PART 1 | The Syria-associated release was a distinct episode

- The October tool depended on keys described as associated with Syrian victims, roughly one thousand keys. [2]
- Bitdefender warned that location identification could be imperfect and could prevent some intended victims from benefiting. [2]
- A tool's population scope can matter as much as its version scope; "available" says little about a specific victim's match.
- Preserve the later broad update as a separate fact, rather than merging different paragraphs into one.

## PART 2 | Decryption is not eradication

- A decryption tool addresses an availability problem in data; it does not by itself remove the intrusion path or validate every system.
- If access came from stolen credentials or exposed remote administration, restoring files does not change those conditions.
- Run separate workstreams: security scope and containment, data recovery, and business and stakeholder validation.
- A clean scan is not proof that recovery is complete, and readable output is not proof the incident is closed.

## PART 2 | What the recovery numbers mean, and do not mean

- The June figures concern prior tool use and estimated ransom payments avoided, not money returned or total economic harm. [4]
- Avoided payment leaves out interruption, response labor, replacement equipment and lost work for an affected organization.
- A recovery count is not a count of unique organizations, devices or users unless the source defines those units. [4]
- Preserve attribution and the period; do not silently upgrade a provider estimate into an audited financial result.
Caption: Evidence grade: Reported provider estimates; not an audited global total.
Notes: Figures and their scope from [4]; the denominator and accounting-category points are original analysis.

## PART 2 | A shutdown claim is not a closure certificate

- McAfee described a May 31, 2019 shutdown announcement and treated the operators' financial boasts skeptically; Bitdefender questioned the claimed revenue. [5][6]
- The study does not present the claimed billions as verified earnings or as proof that every affiliate stopped working.
- A shutdown statement can be useful intelligence while differing from verified arrests, removed malware copies, or ended access.
- Ask which risk actually changed, and name the observed change rather than saying the threat simply vanished.
Caption: Evidence grade: Reported operator claim, kept separate from Confirmed outcomes.
Notes: Shutdown date and skeptical treatment from [5] and [6]; the claimed-versus-confirmed discipline is the study's framing.

## PART 2 | Prevention must reflect more than one access path

- The February 2019 account described remote-access and credential-related incidents among the observed activity. [3]
- That evidence makes an email-only prevention lesson inadequate; review the actual ways users and administrators reach systems.
- Remote administration needs a clear owner, a defined purpose, and exceptions with review dates and an accountable sponsor.
- An account in a log is not a person; investigators need timing, device, session and change context, and should document gaps.

Notes: Remote-access and credential observations from [3]; the identity-investigation and least-rights guidance is original analysis.

## DEFENDER VIEW | A controlled recovery decision, not run-it-on-everything

- Preserve the evidence and an unchanged copy of affected data first; do not experiment against the only remaining copy.
- Verify an authorized, reputable recovery source and record its tool version, documentation date and stated limitations.
- Define a representative validation set that reflects the business process, and record failures and partial successes honestly.
- Require business acceptance; a file can be readable but incomplete, and the owner should confirm it supports a real task.

## CLOSE | Precision makes relief usable, not a single announcement

- GandCrab's lesson is that recovery assistance can be consequential without being universal.
- Precise version identification and preserved evidence decide whether a tool can help a specific case.
- Trustworthy recovery sources, plus separate security and business validation, make relief usable when it arrives.
- The 2018-2019 case is strong enough without extending it through assumptions about rebranding or personal continuity.
