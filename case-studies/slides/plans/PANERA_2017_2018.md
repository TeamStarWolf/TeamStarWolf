## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY | Exposure and disclosure failure, not proven theft

- Panera is a customer-data exposure and vulnerability-disclosure failure, not a documented ransomware operation or a proven database theft.
- A public-facing application can violate confidentiality without malware, a stolen admin account or a disrupted restaurant.
- The central question is what happened between a researcher reporting readable customer data and the company saying it was resolved.
- A company statement establishes what the company said at that time, not what a completed forensic investigation later proved.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed exposure report; no theft adjudicated.
Notes: Executive-assessment framing; sources are [1] through [9], with no customer interface queried here.

## PART 1 | Authentication is not the missing authorization check

- An application can hold authority to read customer data yet fail to check which customer may request which record.
- Authentication asks whether an identity is accepted; authorization asks whether that identity may receive the record.
- Adding a login screen changes the precondition but need not restore a missing ownership check.
- This interprets the reported behavior; the study did not read Panera source code or name the defective function.
Caption: Evidence grade: Inferred analysis of the reported behavior.
Notes: Authorization-versus-authentication discipline drawn from [1] and [2]; the mechanism is analytical, not a source-code finding.

## PART 1 | Predictable IDs amplify scale; they are not proof of theft

- Sequential identifiers can reduce the effort to discover other records when an authorization check is absent.
- Predictability alone does not establish a vulnerability, and replacing identifiers with random strings does not repair a missing rule.
- A last-four-digit value is not the full card number, expiration date and security code.
- No endpoint, customer record or collection script is reproduced; historical research is not a new data-access event.
Caption: Evidence grade: Reported fields; Inferred scale analysis.
Notes: Identifier and payment-fragment reasoning from [1]; the obscurity-versus-access-rule point is the study's analysis.

## PART 2 | Card fragment, contact fields and loyalty IDs differ in risk

- A last-four-digit value can make a fraudulent message look familiar, but it is not a payment-fraud capability on its own.
- Contact and identity fields in combination can make targeted impersonation more convincing.
- A loyalty identifier does not establish that a redemption workflow accepted it without other checks.
- These are plausible consequences of the data category, not documented losses attributed to this incident.

## DEFENDER VIEW | What evidence should close a reported customer-data issue

- Receiving a report, acknowledging it, assigning a ticket, deploying a change and verifying the boundary are separate events.
- A completion claim should identify which of those events actually occurred.
- A verification package ties the reported behavior to a reproducible test and names an owner accountable for scope.
- "No evidence" needs a scope: logging coverage, review period and method, or a reader cannot infer that no access occurred.
Caption: Evidence grade: Inferred standard; proposed, not Panera's documented practice.
Notes: Disclosure-process and verification framing from the study's management sections; absence of a published ticket history is not proof nobody investigated.

- Separate exposed-or-reachable from confirmed-acquired, and separate notified, discovered and disclosed dates.
- "Time from reporter call to containment" is not "time from original report to verified correction"; label both clocks.
- Open gaps: no named actor, no extraction volume, no verified loss total, no reconciled exposure count.
