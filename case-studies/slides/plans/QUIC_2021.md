## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY | A transport redesign, not an HTTP revision

- The May 2021 specs formalized a transport integrating security, multiplexed streams and connection management over UDP.
- RFC 9000 defined the core transport, RFC 9001 the TLS integration, RFC 9002 the recovery and congestion control.
- QUIC is not an HTTP revision, nor a TLS record stream moved unchanged from TCP to UDP.
- It changed the responsibilities of endpoints and networks, and each benefit carried an operational qualification.
Caption: Evidence grade: primary standards and dated records; not an incident with victims.
Notes: Executive-assessment framing from [1][2][3]; separation of transport from HTTP is the central discipline.

## ORIGINS | The 2017 experiment was not the final standard

- The 2017 SIGCOMM paper describes a pre-IETF design built to work around middleboxes and slow OS transport updates.
- Its deployment experience shows the experiment carried real traffic, not that the RFC wire format was already shipped.
- A chart labeled "QUIC" from that period is not automatically a measurement of QUIC version 1.
- Implementation, handshake, congestion behavior and negotiated version are all part of what was measured.
Caption: Evidence grade: pre-IETF research paper, used for motivation and lineage only.
Notes: Design motivation and the versioning caveat from [4]; no experiment reproduced.

## STANDARDIZATION | Standardization included deliberate subtraction

- A contributor account records informal 2015 discussion and a formal 2016 meeting leading to working-group development.
- Early features were removed, including a parity-based forward-error-correction scheme and ACK entropy, when costs outran benefit.
- The handshake moved from Google's QUIC-Crypto toward integration with TLS 1.3.
- An early feature list is a poor proxy for the final design; real implementation experience changed what was retained.
Caption: Evidence grade: first-person contributor account, not final normative text.
Notes: Removals and handshake transition from [5]; preserve the subtractions rather than backfilling them into 2021.

## DEPLOYMENT | A 2020 draft rollout preceded the final RFCs

- Chrome's October 7, 2020 post described a draft h3-29 rollout starting at 25 percent of Stable users.
- It ran alongside Google QUIC Q050 and explicitly stated M85 did not yet support IETF QUIC 0-RTT.
- The announced starting percentage is not evidence of completed worldwide deployment.
- Improvements reported before 0-RTT support cannot all be attributed to that feature, or applied to every network.
Caption: Evidence grade: provider report of a draft rollout, not final-RFC deployment.
Notes: Rollout percentage, Q050 coexistence and the 0-RTT gap from [6]; no current default inferred from it.

## TRANSPORT | What RFC 9000 actually provided

- Version 1 supplies ordered byte streams, bidirectional and unidirectional, with flow control.
- Connection identifiers let a connection survive a path change, including NAT rebinding; active migration is limited to clients here.
- Availability defenses include the anti-amplification limit and a 1,200-byte minimum client Initial UDP payload.
- A connection identifier is not a bypass of network policy, and anti-amplification is not unlimited scalability.
Caption: Evidence grade: confirmed, RFC 9000 normative requirements.
Notes: Migration, address validation and the minimum-payload rule from [1]; mechanisms with purposes and boundaries.

## TLS INTEGRATION | Encryption did not keep the ordinary record stack

- RFC 9001 uses the TLS handshake for authentication and keys while QUIC performs packet protection.
- Handshake messages are carried through QUIC; the protocols cooperate rather than layering TLS over TCP.
- Not every packet is equally protected: Initial keys derive from visible information, and Version Negotiation packets are unprotected.
- An encrypted transport can still expose what is needed to establish and route a connection.
Caption: Evidence grade: confirmed, RFC 9001 normative requirements.
Notes: Packet-protection model and the unprotected packet types from [2]; distinguish packet type and connection stage.

## RECOVERY | QUIC supplies its own delivery over UDP

- Packet numbers increase within a number space and are not reused, separating an original transmission from a later one.
- Information needing reliable delivery is sent in new packets, avoiding retransmission ambiguity.
- A probe-timeout expiration does not by itself require collapsing the congestion window or declaring persistent congestion.
- "UDP is unreliable" is insufficient: the protocol above UDP supplies its own delivery and recovery, at the cost of endpoint state.
Caption: Evidence grade: confirmed, RFC 9002; the study performed no independent validation.
Notes: Packet-number spaces and the probe-timeout distinction from [3]; reliability is not free.

## INVARIANTS | A stable invariant is not a version habit

- RFC 8999 defines the small set of properties intended to stay common across QUIC versions.
- It describes long and short header forms and a version field in the long header.
- A parser that treats one version's incidental behavior as permanent can obstruct later evolution.
- Compatibility claims need a defined scope; recognizing a version-1 layout is not handling all future versions.
Caption: Evidence grade: confirmed, RFC 8999 normative scope.
Notes: Invariant purpose and header definitions from [7]; the 2017 paper already made evolution a central concern [4].

## HTTP/3 | A separate published mapping, dated 2022

- RFC 9114 (June 2022) maps HTTP semantics onto QUIC, assigning a request-response pair to a stream.
- It uses a dedicated control stream and replaces HTTP/2's HPACK with QPACK because ordering assumptions differ.
- Independent streams keep one stream's loss from imposing its transport-ordering requirement on another.
- QUIC is the transport; HTTP/3 is an application protocol using it; the 2022 publication date is a standards milestone.
Caption: Evidence grade: confirmed, RFC 9114, June 2022, later context.
Notes: Mapping, QPACK and stream independence from [8]; 2020 draft deployments are compatible with the 2022 RFC.

## EARLY DATA | 0-RTT moves responsibility into the application

- RFC 9308 (September 2022) stresses that applications must decide what may be sent in 0-RTT.
- Replayed early data can cause repeated processing; a harmless-looking operation can differ when combined into a sequence.
- UDP blocking can force another transport or a connection failure; falling back to TLS over TCP is not falling back to cleartext.
- The review question is whether repeated processing can change a durable result, not merely whether early bytes were encrypted.
Caption: Evidence grade: later Informational guidance, RFC 9308.
Notes: Replay, fallback and the application-semantics point from [9]; the durable-record case is an analytical example, not a QUIC exploit.

## NETWORK VISIBILITY | Signals shifted, they did not vanish

- RFC 9312 (September 2022, Informational) notes the optional spin bit can support passive RTT estimation when endpoints enable it.
- Blocking a flow early differs from admitting a handshake then dropping later packets, which can cause loss and expensive timeouts.
- RFC 9221 (March 2022) adds DATAGRAM frames that share the crypto context, carry no stream identifier, and are not retransmitted on loss.
- Some familiar transport signals are protected or changed; "QUIC is invisible" and "QUIC makes everything reliable" both overgeneralize.
Caption: Evidence grade: later Informational and extension RFCs, 9312 and 9221.
Notes: Spin bit and filtering from [10]; datagram behavior from [11]; encrypting metadata shifts, not removes, operator visibility.

## CROSS-CUTTING THEMES | Overstated versus understated in the retelling

Left: Easy to overstate
- "QUIC is just faster HTTP", collapsing the transport and the application protocol.
- Treating a 2020 draft rollout as final-RFC worldwide deployment or the current default.
- Reading "encrypted transport" as every packet and all handshake metadata hidden.
Right: Easy to understate
- An integrated security architecture, not a TLS record stream moved unchanged onto UDP.
- Deliberate removals, including FEC and ACK entropy, that shaped the final design.
- 2022 extensions and guidance that are later context, not part of the May 2021 package.
Caption: Evidence grade: editorial corrections bounded to each source's scope.
Notes: Overstated and understated contrasts drawn from [1][2][4][5][6]; keep standard, deployment and default distinct.

## CLOSE | A standard is not a deployment or a default

- QUIC's 2021 milestone was a transport redesign with an explicit security architecture, not a single speed feature.
- Standard published, deployed and default are three separate claims, and 2022 material is a later extension.
- QUIC does not remove the need for endpoint security or certificate validation; encrypting metadata shifts, not removes, visibility.
- The most valuable next step is a reproducible comparison of named implementations and negotiated versions under documented conditions.
Caption: Evidence grade: case-specific conclusions, bounded to the record.
Notes: Conclusion and research limits from [1] through [11]; requirements, provider reports and analysis stay explicitly separate.
