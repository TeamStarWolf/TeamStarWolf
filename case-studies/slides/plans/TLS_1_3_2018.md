## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY | A standard is a specification, not a deployment

- TLS 1.3 is an advancement in a widely used protocol, not a breach, campaign or outage.
- Security properties have boundaries: encryption protects a channel between its endpoints.
- It does not repair server misconfiguration, prove a site is honest, or make repeated actions harmless.
- Faster establishment adds a decision about which data may arrive before the ordinary handshake finishes.
Caption: Evidence grade: constructive standards record, not a named-victim reconstruction.
Notes: Executive-assessment framing from [1][2]; no adoption measurement or service certification asserted.

## KEY FACTS | Standard, deployed, and default are three claims

Stat: 2018 | RFC 8446 published, Standards Track

Stat: 9 | Primary IETF or RFC Editor records
Stat: 0 | Live TLS configurations changed
- Support is a capability; negotiation is an observed result; the default is a separate choice.
- A deployed system is a library version, a configuration, a certificate and an application together.
- Ask which connections actually negotiate TLS 1.3, not merely which services support it.
- The study claims no browser market share and no universal product-support figure.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed, dated RFC and IETF records.
Notes: Record counts and the capability-versus-result distinction from [1][2][3].

## PART 1 | What RFC 8446 actually changed in 2018

- Record protection restricted to authenticated-encryption (AEAD) constructions.
- Static RSA and static Diffie-Hellman key exchange removed; cipher-suite selection separated out.
- More handshake content encrypted after ServerHello; a revised key schedule introduced.
- Optional early data added; fresh ephemeral exchange supports forward secrecy, PSK-only and early data need narrower claims.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed from RFC 8446.
Notes: Change set from [1]; removing an option can be a security improvement by reducing combinations to get right.

## PART 1 | A 2026 replacement, still TLS 1.3, not TLS 1.4

- RFC 8446, authored by Eric Rescorla, published August 2018 as the historical anchor.
- RFC 9846, published July 2026, obsoletes RFC 8446 while still specifying TLS version 1.3.
- This is a replacement specification, not evidence the protocol became TLS 1.4.
- The study separates the original 2018 advancement from later standards context; research cutoff 15 November 2026.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed RFC Editor record.
Notes: Status correction from [1][2]; a later document should not rewrite what happened in 2018.

## PART 1 | Encryption protects a channel, not everything

- The specification treats authentication, confidentiality and integrity as channel goals.
- TLS does not inherently hide transmitted lengths; applications must define their own use and identity checks.
- Draw the real endpoints: a browser to a delivery network, then a separate link to an origin.
- Logs, caches and databases retain content after an endpoint receives it; those are different failure modes.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed channel properties; the boundary drawing is original analysis.
Notes: Channel model from [2]; avoid both "encryption solves everything" and dismissing a narrow, testable control.

## PART 1 | Forward secrecy needs a qualified claim

- Forward secrecy limits what a later compromise of long-term secrets reveals about earlier traffic, under the right key exchange.
- It is not a promise plaintext never existed, nor that a compromised endpoint cannot read current activity.
- Review which mode actually occurs during a full connection and during resumption.
- Exporting traffic secrets into broadly accessible logs creates a separate disclosure path; a version number is not post-quantum protection.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed property with qualified scope; operational guidance is original analysis.
Notes: Qualified explanation from [1][2]; state the assurance that applies to the traffic discussed, not the strongest configuration.

## PART 2 | Early data trades latency for replay risk

- RFC 8470, September 2018, addresses HTTP use of early data.
- Replay can occur without the client's awareness; the Early-Data field and 425 Too Early coordinate across hops.
- Waiting for a later handshake does not retroactively make a request safe if it arrived as early data earlier.
- Do not approve an endpoint by HTTP method alone; review actual side effects before allowing early data.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed RFC 8470 behavior; the decision-record guidance is original analysis.
Notes: Early-data mechanics from [4]; keeping early data disabled while using ordinary TLS 1.3 is a legitimate stage.

## PART 2 | Identity checking stays an application job

- RFC 9525, November 2023, gives standards guidance for service identity in TLS.
- A certificate chain can be technically valid while the client connects to the wrong name.
- Test both trust-path validation and identity matching; a successful connection alone proves neither.
- Do not normalize certificate-bypass instructions; restrict any diagnostic exception and remove it before acceptance.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed RFC 9525 guidance; the test framing is original analysis.
Notes: Identity obligation from [5]; this study does not recommend weakening identity checks for compatibility.

## PART 2 | Middleboxes shaped what could deploy

- RFC 8701, an Informational RFC of January 2020, documents GREASE.
- GREASE reserves values implementations advertise to exercise tolerance of unknown options.
- A network component that assumes handshakes must resemble older traffic can reject newer behavior.
- Test through the actual path, including enterprise proxies and managed gateways, not only a direct lab server.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed Informational RFC; the deployment framing is original analysis.
Notes: GREASE and extensibility from [8]; an extension point stays useful only if implementations keep exercising it.

## PART 2 | QUIC is related, not interchangeable

- RFC 9001, May 2021, specifies how TLS secures QUIC.
- QUIC uses TLS-derived keys and handshake messages but protects packets itself, not via the ordinary TLS record layer.
- TLS 1.3, QUIC and HTTP/3 are related technologies at different layers, not one upgrade.
- A measurement plan should name the transport and application protocol actually used, and compare like for like.
Caption: Evidence grade: Confirmed RFC 9001; the measurement caution is original analysis.
Notes: QUIC relationship from [9]; a newer transport does not remove authorization, request-semantics or safe-logging obligations.

## DEFENDER VIEW | Inventory endpoints, then stage the migration

Left: Inventory termination points
- Map public edges, load balancers, reverse proxies, gateways and direct listeners.
- Record which team, library and configuration controls each point.
- Include non-browser clients and keep unknown endpoints visible, not excused.
Right: Stage the migration with gates
- Gate one: supported implementation and a documented target configuration.
- Gates two and three: representative staging test, then limited production rollout with clear failure signals.
- Do not bundle early data, certificate automation and a new transport into the first change.
Caption: Evidence grade: Original recommendations drawn from the study.
Notes: Termination map and staged gates from the original recommendations; define rollback narrowly and avoid re-enabling obsolete versions.

## CROSS-CUTTING THEMES | Where the record is overstated or understated

Left: Easy to overstate
- "Encrypted all the way" hides a place where an intermediary sees plaintext.
- Reading a supported version as a negotiated one, or a benchmark average as the default path.
- Claiming the 2018 standard alone fixes the application or delivers post-quantum protection.
Right: Easy to understate
- Removing weak options and encrypting more handshake content is a real security gain.
- Early data is a distinct replay decision deserving its own approval.
- Resumption is a lifecycle and secret-handling responsibility, not a free performance switch.
Caption: Evidence grade: editorial corrections the case requires.
Notes: Overstatement and understatement contrasts from [1][2][4]; keep standard, deployed and default as separate claims.

## CLOSE | Precision beats a padlock or a version number

- A standard published, widely deployed, and enabled by default are three different claims.
- TLS 1.3 improves a foundational channel and removes obsolete choices; it does not make every application safe.
- Early data deserves its own decision; identity checks remain; intermediaries change trust boundaries.
- The 2026 replacement is a reminder that an important publication stays part of a living technical ecosystem.
Caption: Evidence grade: case-specific conclusions, scope kept explicit.
Notes: Closure synthesis across [1][2]; a completion record that says only "TLS 1.3 is on" is too imprecise.
